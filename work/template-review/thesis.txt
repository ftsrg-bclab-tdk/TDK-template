The TDK lifecycle

Plan the phases in Table D.1 backwards from the oﬀicial dates in Chapter H: the upload date is fixed, so
every step before it is that date minus the time the rest needs. Each step has an owner, a condition that says
when it is done, and a link to the guidance for it. We track every student’s progress in the same steps, so at the
weekly consultation (Chapter E) we look at the row you are in and the row you are heading for. Some steps
are marked a hill climb: you repeat them, scoring the work, fixing its weakest point and scoring again, until
the row’s condition says stop. Set \lintphase in thesis.tex to your phase’s name in lower case, from
topic to submit; the lint’s checks come due by phase (Section I.2). After the upload, leave it at submit:
the paper is submitted and needs no further checks. Inspect the reviewer PDF every week. It is the version
reviewers will read.
Three steps are ANCHORs: the topic manifest, the spine and the full draft. An anchor is an artifact we approve
before the next phase, and everything after it is measured against it. The topic manifest and the spine are
one page each; we approve the full draft at a revision recorded in the consultation summary. Each anchor
narrows the topic: before the manifest it is a direction, after the spine it is a fixed set of claims with fixed
names, and after the full draft it is a paper that only gets polished. Nothing stops at an anchor, but work that
contradicts one is drift, and drift is the most expensive mistake in the process, because everything built on
the old anchor has to be redone. If an anchor turns out to be wrong, say so at the next consultation and we
change it together; a claim changed silently costs weeks when a reviewer finds the seam.

are fixed, and missing one ends that semester’s TDK. Green rows marked DEPARTMENT are the department’s
sessions, where every TDK student of the department comes and the last problems get fixed in company; we

D.1 Reviewer passes
Two reviewers each spend an evening reading and scoring the paper against the review sheet (Section H.5);
the section jury, mostly from other fields, hears your presentation and reads little else. These are different
readings, and a paper that satisfies you and us can still fail one of them. Prepare for each with a reviewer
pass: an agent in your paper repository reads the paper as a simulated reader with a narrow brief and reports
what that reader would hold against it. A pass takes minutes, so run it early and repeat it. Personas can miss
problems or invent them, so treat their output as objections to check, not as an assessment. Section I.3 says
how to run them.


The skim pass. The template aims to earn the maximum score from a reviewer who reads only the outline,
figures, labelled items and coloured boxes, because a tired reviewer on the second evening reads exactly that.
Test this with a skim view made from the reviewer PDF, which paper CI builds on every push. It keeps the
title, abstract, labelled items and boxes verbatim. Headings retain their page and length; figures retain their
captions; tables retain their captions and a thumbnail. Paragraphs, equations, listings and plain lists become
length markers, such as “paragraph, 7 lines”.
The skim reviewer reads only this view and the review sheet. It scores each criterion and, for any shortfall,
names the item that should have made the point clear. For example, a guarantee may lack a row in the
correctness table. During polish, repeat the pass and fix what it names. Aim for the maximum on every
criterion, but stop after three passes, or earlier once we accept the remaining objections: past that point the
fixes cost more than the points they recover. This is the review sheet hill climb for a reader who skims.


The panel. Table D.2 lists the expert personas. Their responsibilities are disjoint: each concern has one
owner and one place for an answer, so two personas never argue the same point and you never get the same
objection twice in different words. A persona that notices a concern outside its remit hands it to the owner
in one line, without arguing it.
Five rules define these responsibilities. The cryptographer and formal methods expert handle claims inside
the formal model, and the formal methods expert owns the choice of abstraction: what the model leaves
out and whether leaving it out is safe. The networking and cybersecurity expert owns the attacks the paper
excludes. The mean mathematician checks pen-and-paper proofs; the formal methods expert checks tool-
assisted proofs. The systems modelling expert checks system structure; the process modelling expert checks
behaviour. The distributed systems expert checks which faults and scales are studied; the empiricist checks
how they are measured. Every persona checks overclaiming in its own area. Only the champion looks for
results stated more weakly than the evidence supports.
The first four personas read every paper. The others read papers that touch their field.

Appendix E

Weekly consultations

Find a recurring slot. Agree on a weekly slot with us and create a recurring MS Teams event.


Why every week. A week is short enough that a wrong turn costs days, not a month, and long enough to
have something to show. Come even when little has moved: a blocked week is exactly the one we can help
with, and the one students tend to skip.


Why one-on-one. A group meeting averages over everyone’s problem, and yours is specific: your code,
your draft, your plot. Alone with you we read the actual artifact and ask back until the misunderstanding is
found, which takes minutes one-on-one and rarely happens in a group. It also lets us be frank about scope
and effort, which is not a conversation for an audience.


Why in person. In a room we share a whiteboard, which is where notation, architecture and the leading
figure actually get drawn. We also see where you hesitate, which a call hides, and the hard conversations,
when a topic has to be cut or re-aimed, go better face to face.


Consultation slides are optional. A few slides on what you did this week, or slides that make your
work easier for us to understand, save the first ten minutes of every consultation; we welcome them, LLM-
generated included. A useful outline:

   1. what you did since the last consultation, with evidence: a figure, a result, a page of text;
   2. what you read: the papers you added to Zotero this week, one sentence each on why they matter;
   3. what is blocked or where you are unsure;
   4. what you plan for next week;
   5. the questions you need us to decide.


Close with a summary. End by saying back in your own words what we agreed: the decisions, your next
steps and what we owe you. Then save the same as a short written summary in your notes repository, as
notes/consultations/YYYY-MM-DD*.md. If your summary differs from what we meant, we catch it now
rather than a week of work later. The summaries are also where anchor approvals and your decisions on
reviewer objections are recorded, so they are the paper’s memory as much as ours.


                                                    34
The mirror principle. We put in what you put in, and we are open about it. A student who works hard on
their topic gets a lot of our time, our contacts and our help beyond the paper; a student who has put the topic
on the back burner gets less of it. This is not about struggling: a student who struggles and keeps showing
up gets more help, not less. It means we are mindful of where our time goes, and that you decide how much
of it you get. On a separate matter, we value consistency over bursts. A student who is diligent every week
and not very productive gets further than one who disappears for a month and then does brilliant work for
a weekend, because research compounds: each week’s result is what the next week builds on, and a weekly
rhythm keeps the topic alive in your head and in ours.


Recordings and Teams calls. Hold the consultation as a Teams call even when we sit in one room, and
record it with transcription. You can then rewatch the details, feed the transcript to your AI tools and turn it
into the notes in your repository. We recommend Whisper large-v3-turbo over Teams’ built-in transcription:

F.1 Plan before you write: problem to skeleton
Plan the argument before writing prose. Work through the Research and Plan rows of Table D.1 in order,
building on each previous step; after the spine come the figures, the skeleton and the beats.

Problem Start with one paragraph: who has the problem, what goes wrong today, and a concrete scenario
     showing it. This scenario becomes the running example; the paragraph becomes the first page of the
     introduction (content/01-introduction.tex). Then start the literature review. If you cannot yet
     write the paragraph, bring your notes to the next consultation and we will write it together.
Spine One page fixing the paper’s claims, terms and notation. Check every chapter, figure and review
     against it to catch drift, such as terms, symbols or claims changing between chapters.
Skeleton mind map Plan each chapter as paragraph topic sentences with pointers to supporting evidence.
      Check the argument before expanding those sentences into prose. A mind map on paper or in a tool is a
      quick way to do this. Figure F.1.1 shows the expected detail for the guide’s fictional paper. Figure F.1.2
      shows the template’s generic beats in the same form. Use this structure to build a skeleton for your
      own topic.
Running example Choose one scenario to revisit in every chapter. Name it with \exname in thesis.tex
    and introduce it in the introduction (content/01-introduction.tex). Use a runningexample
    box in each chapter to show what its concepts, components, measurements or competitors do with the
    scenario. The boxes alone should explain the paper.
Beats Plan what each paragraph establishes, its evidence, what the example shows and how it leads to the
     next paragraph. Chapter G gives generic beats for every template heading. Replace them with topic-
     specific beats at the start of each planned paragraph: \beat{establishes}{evidence}{running
     example}{leads to}. These print as todos and disappear from the reviewer PDF.

F.3 The leading figure (vezérábra)
The leading figure (the leading figure (content/01-introduction.tex)) shows the problem, your contri-
butions and the result in one picture. Decide what the reader should learn, then draw it and write a caption
that makes it understandable on its own. Use the ours TikZ style to colour all your components blue. The
problem is red, the result green, and reused or external parts grey (prior). Every work has at least two
components; give all of yours the same blue and explain the colours in the caption. If ownership is unclear,
a reviewer may attribute only the smallest part to you.

What we like.

    • Strong papers from strong venues, typically recent. Use peer-reviewed journal and conference

A topic sometimes yields two TDK papers in the same year: you write both, or you and a fellow student
write neighbouring parts of one project, such as a system in one paper and its formal verification in the other.
Each paper is then the other’s companion paper, and three rules hold.

    • Never cite or link it. It gets no bibliography entry and no Overleaf, GitHub or portal link. Citing it
      is very bad practice. The two papers are reviewed at the same time, each on its own, so the reviewer
      scores this paper against a source that is not published and that nobody has reviewed yet, and the
      citation suggests one contribution cut in two.
    • Call it a companion paper. Where the reader needs to know where this paper ends, one sentence
      says what the other one does, without its title: “A companion paper verifies the registry’s revocation
      protocol in Tamarin; this paper builds the registry and measures it.”
    • Restate what this paper needs from it. The core part this paper relies on, such as a system model,
      its definitions or a protocol, goes into the background chapter (Section G.2), condensed to what this
      paper uses, so that the paper reads without the other. It is background here even if you wrote it, so
      claim each result in only one of the two papers. Only when the page limit leaves no room does it go
      into an appendix; the background chapter then keeps a paragraph that summarises it and points there.

The companion rule of the lint (Section I.2) fails on a citation of a TDK paper of the same year; on a link
to an Overleaf project, to a paper of this year’s TDK on the portal or to the repository of a TDK paper of
this year other than this paper’s own, the first repository the implementation chapter cites or links; and on a
citation or link attached to the words “companion paper”.

I.3 Running the reviewer passes
Self-score and reviewer passes. The paper repository carries two agent skills in .claude/skills/, so
each commit has the skills that review it. On every push, paper CI uploads the evidence of that commit
as one artifact: both PDFs, the build logs, the lint output and the skim view, kept for 14 days. .claude/
skills/self-score/ scores the artifact’s reviewer.pdf A–E using the review sheet’s wording and page
references, and identifies the weakest criterion and cheapest fix. .claude/skills/reviewer-passes/
runs the skim reviewer and expert panel on the same artifact. scripts/review/review.py fetches the
artifact of a named commit and checks its hashes. Self-score writes nothing; the reviewer passes write
nothing unless you record a run. Section D.1 says what the passes are and when to run them; this section
says how.


Running them. Push your commit and wait for paper CI, which builds the evidence of every push (Sec-
tion I.2). Then, in your paper repository, ask your agent to run the reviewer passes, or name one persona:
“review as the cryptographer”. The skill in .claude/skills/reviewer-passes/ fetches the CI artifact
of that commit with scripts/review/review.py, which refuses an artifact whose hashes do not match. It
gives each persona a directory holding only that persona’s inputs: the reviewer PDF, the skim view, or both,
and your spine if you name the file. Without a spine, a persona judges overclaiming against the abstract and
the introduction. Each persona runs apart from the others and never sees another persona’s findings or an
earlier run. The chat response gives each persona’s worst finding, the criterion and page, and the cheapest
fix. It then lists the three cheapest changes overall. Ask a persona for more to see its remaining findings.


Recording a run. By default the skill writes no files and never edits the paper. You choose the fixes, and
the consultation summary (Chapter E) records your decisions. We record a run at three milestones (the
spine premortem, the full draft and before upload), and you may record any run you choose. A recorded run
becomes one issue on your paper repository: the panel, the skim scores, one checkbox per finding, and the
hashes of the artifact and of each persona’s inputs. Propose a disposition for each finding in a comment on
the issue: F3 fixed in <sha>, F4 rejected: <reason> or F5 deferred. We confirm the dispositions
with the label review-confirmed. The issue is not an input to the next run, and the skill keeps no memory
between runs. Rerun it on the revised paper to check whether the fixes answer the objections.

L.2 A thesis from this paper
A TDK paper can become a BSc or MSc thesis.               The required files are kept as com-
mented inputs in thesis.tex: the thesis title page (include/titlepage.tex), student declaration
(include/declaration.tex), faculty structure guide (include/guideline.tex) and task description
(include/project.tex). Set \vikdoktipus to \bsc or \msc, then make the following changes from the
faculty’s guide:

    • Length. Our guidance, as the guideline gives no page count: a BSc thesis is around 30–45 pages, an
      MSc thesis 60–90 pages of useful content; ask us, departments differ.
    • Language. The faculty’s default is Hungarian. English suits students aiming at international research
      or industry, but takes more writing effort and more reading effort from advisors and reviewers. This
      template is English-only. For a Hungarian thesis, use the faculty’s template.
    • Format. The guideline: A4, 2.5 cm margins plus 1 cm for binding, and by default 12 pt Times New
      Roman with one-and-a-half line spacing, from which small deviations and other typefaces are allowed.
