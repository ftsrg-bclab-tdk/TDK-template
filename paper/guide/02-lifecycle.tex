\chapter{The TDK lifecycle}\label{guide:lifecycle}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Plan the phases in \cref{guide:tab:lifecycle} backwards from the official dates in \cref{guide:tdk}: the upload date is fixed, so every step before it is that date minus the time the rest needs. Each step has an owner, a condition that says when it is done, and a link to the guidance for it.
We track every student's progress in the same steps, so at the weekly consultation (\cref{guide:consult}) we look at the row you are in and the row you are heading for. Some steps are marked \emph{a hill climb}: you repeat them, scoring the work, fixing its weakest point and scoring again, until the row's condition says stop.
Set \texttt{\textbackslash lintphase} in \texttt{draft.tex} to your phase's name in lower case, from \texttt{topic} to \texttt{submit}; the lint's checks come due by phase (\cref{guide:code:paperci}). The date raises it whatever it says: from 1 October the lint works at \texttt{draft} at least, from 22 October at \texttt{polish}, and from 30 October at \texttt{submit}, so a paper is never checked at an earlier phase than the calendar's.
Inspect the final PDF every week. It is the version reviewers will read.

Three steps are \anchor{}s: the topic manifest, the spine and the full draft. An anchor is an artifact we approve before the next phase, and everything after it is measured against it. The topic manifest and the spine are one page each; we approve the full draft at a revision recorded in the consultation summary. Each anchor narrows the topic: before the manifest it is a direction, after the spine it is a fixed set of claims with fixed names, and after the full draft it is a paper that only gets polished. Nothing stops at an anchor, but work that contradicts one is drift, and drift is the most expensive mistake in the process, because everything built on the old anchor has to be redone. If an anchor turns out to be wrong, say so at the next consultation and we change it together; a claim changed silently costs weeks when a reviewer finds the seam.

Red rows mark anchors. Yellow rows marked \official{} are the faculty's steps: their portal dates (\cref{guide:tdk:dates}) are fixed, and missing one ends that semester's TDK. Green rows marked \department{} are the department's sessions, where every TDK student of the department comes and the last problems get fixed in company; we organise them.

\section{Reviewer passes}\label{guide:lifecycle:reviewers}
Two reviewers each spend an evening reading and scoring the paper against the review sheet (\cref{guide:tdk:sheet}); the section jury, mostly from other fields, hears your presentation and reads little else. These are different readings, and a paper that satisfies you and us can still fail one of them. Prepare for each with a \emph{reviewer pass}: an agent in your paper repository reads the paper as a simulated reader with a narrow brief and reports what that reader would hold against it. A pass takes minutes, so run it early and repeat it. Personas can miss problems or invent them, so treat their output as objections to check, not as an assessment. \Cref{guide:code:reviewers} says how to run them.

\paragraph{The skim pass.} The template aims to earn the maximum score from a reviewer who reads only the outline, figures, labelled items and coloured boxes, because a tired reviewer on the second evening reads exactly that. Test this with a \emph{skim view} made from the final PDF, which paper CI builds on main-branch pushes, non-draft pull requests and manual runs. It keeps the title, abstract, labelled items and boxes verbatim. Headings retain their page and length; figures retain their captions; tables retain their captions and a thumbnail. Paragraphs, equations, listings and plain lists become length markers, such as \enquote{\P{} 7 lines}.

The skim reviewer reads only this view and the review sheet. It scores each criterion and, for any shortfall, names the item that should have made the point clear. For example, a guarantee may lack a row in the correctness table. During polish, repeat the pass and fix what it names. Aim for the maximum on every criterion, but stop after three passes, or earlier once we accept the remaining objections: past that point the fixes cost more than the points they recover. This is the review sheet hill climb for a reader who skims.

\paragraph{The panel.} \Cref{guide:tab:reviewers} lists the expert personas. Their responsibilities are \emph{disjoint}: each concern has one owner and one place for an answer, so two personas never argue the same point and you never get the same objection twice in different words. A persona that notices a concern outside its remit hands it to the owner in one line, without arguing it.

Five rules define these responsibilities. The cryptographer and formal methods expert handle claims inside the formal model, and the formal methods expert owns the choice of abstraction: what the model leaves out and whether leaving it out is safe. The networking and cybersecurity expert owns the attacks the paper excludes. The mean mathematician checks pen-and-paper proofs; the formal methods expert checks tool-assisted proofs. The systems modelling expert checks system structure; the process modelling expert checks behaviour. The distributed systems expert checks which faults and scales are studied; the empiricist checks how they are measured. Every persona checks overclaiming in its own area. Only the champion looks for results stated more weakly than the evidence supports.

The first four personas read every paper. The others read papers that touch their field.

\paragraph{When.}\label{guide:lifecycle:when-passes} Run the passes at four steps of \cref{guide:tab:lifecycle}. First, use the panel for the spine's premortem: predict the objections to the claims and answer them in the plan, while changing a claim still costs a sentence rather than a chapter. Second, have every relevant persona read the draft, and fix each blocker or acknowledge it in the limitations before the full draft reaches us, so that our reading goes to what only we can see. Third, repeat the skim pass during polish. Fourth, after the deadline, turn the remaining panel objections into the reviewer questions you rehearse answers to.

\section{Requirements}\label{guide:lifecycle:polish}
The words \emph{must}, \emph{we recommend} and \emph{you can} mean what \cref{guide:here:words} says.

\Cref{guide:tab:polish} lists the requirements, in the order they come due. A rule name identifies an automated check that supports the row; it does not establish the whole requirement. You must read every row yourself. Count rules such as \texttt{placeholders} and \texttt{beats} report remaining work without blocking. The lint's rows are checked by \texttt{make check-final}, which runs every rule against the final PDF (\cref{guide:code:paperci}). The paper CI runs the same rules on main-branch pushes, non-draft pull requests and manual dispatch, so by the last week they should have been green for a while: a red row in the last week is a surprise, and the last week has no room for surprises. The polish phase is for checking, not writing: if a row needs more than an hour, it belonged to the draft phase, and the full draft was not full. A row that restates a step of \cref{guide:tab:lifecycle} only names the step and points there. Before upload, run \texttt{make check-submit}, which runs the lint at that phase whatever \texttt{\textbackslash lintphase} says (from 30 October the date does the same for every run), and go through every row due by \texttt{submit}.
