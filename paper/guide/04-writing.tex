\section{Plan before you write: problem to skeleton}\label{guide:writing:spine}
Plan the argument before writing prose. Work through the Research and Plan rows of \cref{guide:tab:lifecycle} in order, building on each previous step; after the spine come the figures, the skeleton and the beats.
\begin{description}
  \item[Problem] Start with one paragraph: who has the problem, what goes wrong today, and a concrete scenario showing it. This scenario becomes the running example; the paragraph becomes the first page of \tplref{ch:intro}. Then start the literature review. If you cannot yet write the paragraph, bring your notes to the next consultation and we will write it together.
  \item[Spine] One page fixing the paper's claims, terms and notation. Check every chapter, figure and review against it to catch drift, such as terms, symbols or claims changing between chapters.
  \item[Skeleton mind map] Plan each chapter as paragraph topic sentences with pointers to supporting evidence. Check the argument before expanding those sentences into prose. A mind map on paper or in a tool is a quick way to do this. \Cref{guide:fig:mindmap} shows the expected detail for the guide's fictional paper. \Cref{guide:fig:template-skeleton} shows the template's generic beats in the same form. Use this structure to build a skeleton for your own topic.
  \item[Running example] Choose one scenario to revisit in every chapter. Name it with \texttt{\textbackslash exname} in \texttt{draft.tex} and introduce it in \tplref{ch:intro}. Use a \texttt{runningexample} box in each chapter to show what its concepts, components, measurements or competitors do with the scenario. The boxes alone should explain the paper.
  \item[Beats] Plan what each paragraph establishes, its evidence, what the example shows and how it leads to the next paragraph. \Cref{guide:chapters} gives generic beats for every template heading. Replace them with topic-specific beats at the start of each planned paragraph: \texttt{\textbackslash beat\{establishes\}\{evidence\}\{running example\}\{leads to\}}. These print as todos and disappear from the reviewer PDF.
\end{description}

\section{The essentials}\label{guide:writing:essentials}
The list below defines the elements expected in every paper of this kind, including the problem, research rationale, requirements, leading figure, threat model, evaluation target, research questions, gap matrix and summary with evidence. These are the named items in \cref{guide:fig:template-skeleton} that reviewers look for. Each row gives the review-sheet criterion that scores it.

The lint calls these items the \emph{essentials}. Their status appears on the draft PDF's Checks page and at the start of the CI job summary. The list is defined in \texttt{scripts/lint/essentials.lua}; \texttt{make essentials} generates this section from it. The requirements still apply if you delete the skeleton chapters. Each row links to a worked example in \cref{guide:chapters}.

\section{The leading figure (vezérábra)}\label{guide:writing:figure}
The leading figure (\tplref{guide:fig:overview}) shows the problem, your contributions and the result in one picture. Decide what the reader should learn, then draw it and write a caption that makes it understandable on its own. Use the \texttt{ours} TikZ style to colour all your components blue to indicate that they are your contribution. The problem is red, the result green, and reused or external parts grey (\texttt{prior}). Every work has at least two components; give all of yours the same blue and explain the colours in the caption. If ownership is unclear, a reviewer may attribute only the smallest part to you.

Draft the figure during research and keep exploring it. Drawing often helps clarify the argument. \Cref{guide:fig:collage} shows four examples from our group.

\section{An alternative: the paper as a dependency graph}\label{guide:writing:depgraph}
The skeleton mind map (\cref{guide:fig:mindmap}) is a tree of chapters and their paragraphs. Draw an arrow from each paragraph to every paragraph that needs it, and the tree becomes a dependency graph of the paper (\cref{guide:fig:depgraph}). Lay it out in bands, one per chapter in reading order, with each band's paragraphs in their order in the chapter. Colour green the paragraphs that deliver a contribution, tag each with its contribution, and leave the rest white. The green is for the reviewer: before reading a sentence, they see where your contribution is, how much of the paper it fills, what it stands on, and which evaluation question tests it. Green marks paragraphs here, not components, so the architecture figure keeps the blue of \texttt{ours} for the parts of the system you built.

It can take either of two places in the paper. As \tplref{guide:fig:overview}, it suits a work whose contribution is a model, a tool and an evaluation spread over several chapters, which a problem-to-result picture would flatten into one box; the green has to read at a glance, so merge a chapter's paragraphs into its sections when there are many. As the figure of the structure paragraph at the end of the introduction (\cref{guide:chapters:structure}), it replaces the list of chapters: the paragraph says what the bands, arrows and colours mean, and the figure shows the rest.

Drawing it also checks the skeleton before any prose exists. Every arrow should run down to a later chapter or right within one; an arrow pointing up or left is a paragraph that needs something the reader has not reached yet, so move one of the two, or say where the missing part comes. A green box with no path to an evaluation question is a contribution the paper claims but does not back. A background box that no arrow leaves is background the paper does not use. Draw only the arrows a reader needs to follow the argument: the figure shows where the contribution is, not every cross-reference.

\section{Figures}\label{guide:writing:figures}
We recommend TikZ for every figure: drawn in the paper repository, it matches the paper's fonts, scales cleanly and allows late edits; \cref{guide:latex} covers the template's TikZ and pgfplots setup. Sketch in \href{https://www.drawio.com/}{draw.io} while exploring, then redraw in TikZ after agreeing the spine. You can also export draw.io and Inkscape drawings as SVG and include them with \texttt{\textbackslash includesvg} (\cref{guide:latex:figures}), so the text stays sharp. Raster images and screenshots, for example a figure from another paper, are allowed only with a reason, and they are not vector figures. Use any other exported PDF only as a last resort. Generate plots with pgfplots from the CSV written by \texttt{just reproduce}, so measurements update the figures automatically. Label every axis with its unit.

The template keeps the look of all figures in one file, \texttt{figures/style.tex}, and every picture starts with \verb|\begin{tikzpicture}[fig]|. The file holds a palette in which each colour has one meaning, and styles for the recurring shapes and arrows; rename and recolour them for your paper, keeping one meaning per colour in every figure. They sit beside the template's \texttt{ours} and \texttt{prior} styles (\cref{guide:writing:figure}), which still say whose a component is. Each picture lives in its own \texttt{figures/<name>.tex}, which the chapter inputs inside its \texttt{figure} environment; the caption and the label stay in the chapter. Plots start with \verb|\begin{axis}[figplot]|, which gives them one size, font and grid. The base font is \texttt{\textbackslash footnotesize}, which is 10\,pt in this 12\,pt class. With \texttt{\textbackslash scriptsize} every label sits at 8\,pt, under the call's minimum.

Figure text is at least 10\,pt in print, as the call says: \enquote{10 pt alatti betűméret kerülendő címkéken is} (\cref{guide:tdk:form}). The lint flags text below 10\,pt; math sub- and superscripts, which \TeX{} sets smaller than their base, do not count. Where a figure does not fit at 10\,pt, redraw it with fewer labels or split it.

\section{Captions}\label{guide:writing:captions}
Figures and captions alone should let a reader follow the paper. Start each caption with the claim the figure supports. Then give the context needed to understand it: the question answered, the concept measured, assumptions in the setup, or why a curve bends. Avoid repeating information already clear from the axes and legend. Use two or three sentences for a diagram and as many as needed to explain a result. For examples, read the Nature captions in \citet{guide:gulyas2015navigable}; \cref{guide:fig:caption-example} applies this style in the template.

\section{The abstract}\label{guide:writing:abstract}
We require the abstract to explain why the work is worth reading. We read it at registration, then the committee uses it to assign reviewers, who read it first too. Show that the topic is \textbf{relevant}, solving it is \textbf{non-trivial}, the contribution is \textbf{novel} and the result is \textbf{correct}: these are the four things the whole paper argues (\cref{guide:chapters}). Follow \weblink{https://www.nature.com/documents/nature-summary-paragraph.pdf}{Nature's summary paragraph guide}. The template abstract has placeholders for its seven steps.

The faculty asks for Hungarian and English abstracts at registration (\cref{guide:tdk:dates}), but does not require them in the PDF. Both the Kivonat and the Abstract in the PDF are our requirement: we write them with you in the Overleaf project.

\section{The reference list}\label{guide:writing:refs}
The reference list demonstrates your knowledge of the field and affects two review-sheet criteria (\cref{guide:tdk:sheet}). B/1 requires accurate references: \enquote{a szakirodalmi hivatkozások pontosak}. B/2 gives only step 1 to literature drawn from web pages: \enquote{1: ismert ipari gyakorlat (a dolgozat feldolgozta a weblapokról, technikai dokumentációkból közismert ipari megoldásokat)}. Use the guidance below to select sources and repair the draft's bibliography.

\paragraph{What we like.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Strong papers from strong venues, typically recent.} Use peer-reviewed journal and conference papers respected in the field, including both the foundations of your work and current research. Ask us if you are unsure about a venue. The \href{https://portal.core.edu.au/conf-ranks/}{CORE ranking} for conferences and \href{https://www.scimagojr.com/}{SJR quartiles} for journals offer initial guidance, not a verdict.
  \item \textbf{arXiv only with a reason.} The usual reason is that the work is too recent to have been published yet. Otherwise cite the published version.
  \item \textbf{No weak sources.} Do not use encyclopedias, tutorials, vendor marketing or unreviewed posts as evidence. Find and cite the paper, standard or documentation behind the claim.
  \item \textbf{URLs, strictly selected.} Specifications, standards, official documentation and repositories can be primary sources for technical claims. Cite them when they are; prefer the original source to a blog explaining it.
  \item \textbf{Never URLs only.} A bibliography made entirely of web pages remains at the sheet's step 1, regardless of the prose's quality.
  \item \textbf{Footnotes when the URLs pile up.} Put links that merely identify a tool, library, repository or product in footnotes with \texttt{\textbackslash footnote\{\textbackslash url\{\dots\}\}}. Reserve bibliography entries for sources supporting claims. Web sources in the bibliography need the owner, title, URL and access date that the faculty's thesis guideline asks for (\cref{guide:latex:cite}).
  \item \textbf{Fifty is usually plenty.} We expect a TDK paper to need at most 50 references in most cases; above that, the repair pass checks each entry.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The repair pass.} Check the references against the list above when the draft is complete and again in the last week (\cref{guide:tab:polish}). Correct entries in the Zotero group library so others benefit too. Do not edit \texttt{bib/zotero.bib}; refreshing it would overwrite the fixes.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Read what you cite.} Open every source and verify that it supports your claim. This also applies to citations suggested by AI or copied from another bibliography.
  \item \textbf{Preprints.} Search \href{https://dblp.org/}{dblp} for each arXiv title. It lists preprints and published versions separately.
  \item \textbf{Complete entries.} Include authors, title, venue and year; add volume, number and pages for journal articles, and a DOI wherever available. Zotero's \emph{Add Item by Identifier} fetches metadata from a DOI. Use dblp's BibTeX exports as a model for computer-science venues.
  \item \textbf{One entry per work.} Treat a preprint and its published version as one work. Use one name per venue and retain titles in their original language, as the faculty requires.
  \item \textbf{Check a long list.} Above 50 entries, check that each one supports a claim or a fair comparison. There is no target to cut to.
\end{enumerate}
The \texttt{bib} lint rule checks titles, years and authors, access dates for web sources, and bare DOIs (\cref{guide:code:paperci}). The \texttt{sources} rule only advises: it names each arXiv preprint you cite, and warns when the list exceeds 50 entries or contains only web pages. Judging whether each source is suitable and supports the claim is your job; no rule does it.

\paragraph{What needs a citation.}\label{guide:writing:citation-placement}
Cite borrowed claims, definitions, methods, data and results, including those you paraphrase or summarise. Cite the source when you first introduce them, and make clear which later statements come from it. Your own results should point to the section, figure or experiment that supports them. A bibliography entry alone does not tell the reader which claims it supports.

\paragraph{Where to put it.} Place the citation immediately after the claim, term or method it supports. If it supports the whole sentence, put it at the end of the sentence, before the full stop. When a sentence compares sources, attach each citation to its own claim. A citation at the end of a paragraph can cover the whole paragraph if all of it summarises that source and the scope is clear. Do not leave a single citation at the end of a paragraph that mixes several sources or mixes borrowed findings with your own conclusions; cite the borrowed claims where they occur and signal your own interpretation explicitly. Use \texttt{\textbackslash citet} when the author is part of the sentence, and \texttt{\textbackslash cite} for a numbered reference after a claim (\cref{guide:latex:cite}).

\paragraph{One source across several paragraphs.} Cite the source at the start of a sustained discussion, then keep the attribution clear with wording such as \enquote{Their protocol then\ldots} or \enquote{In the same study,\ldots}. Do not repeat the citation in every sentence or paragraph while it remains clear that you are explaining the same source. A paragraph break alone does not require another citation. Cite it again when you return after discussing another source, when a new section starts a fresh discussion, or whenever the reader could reasonably be unsure whose work you mean. Signal the transition to your own analysis. Each direct quotation still needs its own cited attribution and locator, as explained below.

\paragraph{Quoting.} Quote when the exact wording matters, for example a definition or a requirement you analyse. Introduce the passage and explain how it supports your argument. Otherwise paraphrase in your own words and cite the source. Cite each borrowed result, concept, tool or product at first use, however familiar it is. Unmarked quotations count as plagiarism, and criterion B/2 also rewards proper citation. See \cref{guide:latex:quotations} for copyable syntax.

\paragraph{Short and long quotations.} Keep a short phrase or sentence in the paragraph, inside \texttt{\textbackslash enquote\{...\}}; it supplies the quotation marks and handles nested quotations. If a passage would occupy several lines or contains multiple paragraphs, set it apart in the \texttt{sourcequote} environment. It has a grey left rule and a separate attribution line. Do not add outer quotation marks to a block or italicise the whole passage. Preserve paragraph breaks within it.

\paragraph{Always cite the attribution.} Every quotation needs a citation, even when you name its author in the sentence or in the block's attribution. For an inline quotation, put the citation immediately after the closing quotation mark; for a block, include it in the attribution argument. Add the page or page range, or a section or paragraph identifier for an unpaginated source, so the reader can find the passage. An author's name or a bare URL alone is not enough. Check the wording against the source you actually read; do not present a quotation copied from another paper as if you had checked the original.

\paragraph{Errors in the original: sic.} Preserve the source's wording. When an apparent error could be mistaken for your transcription error, place \texttt{[\textbackslash textit\{sic\}]} immediately after it. This means that the error is in the original. Use it sparingly; unfamiliar spelling or an older usage does not by itself need a correction. Never silently fix an error inside a quotation.

\paragraph{Replaced words and omissions.} Put your own insertions or replacements in square brackets, for example replacing an unclear pronoun with \enquote{[the verifier]}. Bracket a changed initial letter too, as in \enquote{[T]he verifier}. Use \texttt{[\textbackslash ldots]} for an omission within a passage. Preserve the source's meaning and qualifications: removing a condition can turn a faithful excerpt into a false claim. If substantial rewriting is needed, paraphrase and cite instead. Mark any emphasis you add with \enquote{emphasis added} in the attribution.

\paragraph{Web pages.} The faculty guideline in \texttt{include/guideline.tex} requires: \enquote{Internetes hivatkozások megadásakor fontos, hogy az elérési út előtt megadjuk az oldal tulajdonosát és tartalmát (mivel a link egy idő után akár elérhetetlenné is válhat), valamint az elérés időpontját.} Use a \texttt{misc} entry. Set the author to the page owner in double braces, such as \texttt{author = \{\{W3C\}\}}, to keep its name intact. Give the page a descriptive title, a \texttt{url} and a \texttt{urldate} recording when you read it. A citation management tool, like Zotero, takes care of all of that for you.

\paragraph{Where the entries come from: Zotero.} Add sources to the group library with the browser's \href{https://www.zotero.org/download/connectors}{Zotero Connector}. Click it on the paper or DOI page, then correct the fetched metadata in Zotero. Do not write literature entries by hand; the hand-written entries in \texttt{bib/references.bib} are the Zenodo artifact and web pages that are not in Zotero (\cref{guide:latex:cite}).

\section{Disclose AI use}\label{guide:writing:ai}
This template follows the faculty's \href{https://vik.bme.hu/page/1346/}{guidelines on the use of AI}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Declare every use} in the faculty declaration (\tplref{aidecl}, after the bibliography), which the template prints in our English translation because the faculty publishes the form only in Hungarian. Its note lists what to state for each use. The declaration is mandatory for every thesis, and we require it for TDK papers too.
  \item \textbf{Cite AI answers only in the declaration table}, never in the bibliography. This is our rule, and it departs from the faculty's wording, which asks for the MLA entry (a prompt summary, the model and version, the company, the date and a link) both next to the fact and in the bibliography: the declaration table already records the prompt, tool, version and date of every use, and a bibliography is for sources a reader can check. The declaration's note gives the faculty's MLA form. Cite a fact or conclusion you take from an AI answer to the primary source that verifies it.
\end{itemize}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Keep the essential work your own.} The supervisor decides whether enough of the results are yours. A submission will not be approved if AI use is disproportionately large or touches the essential parts of the work. The faculty states: \enquote{If the task was to develop software and most of it was produced with generative AI, this is not acceptable}. AI-supported software serving your own algorithm, procedure or analysis is acceptable.
\end{itemize}
You are responsible for verifying every AI answer; it cannot stand as a reference by itself. \Cref{guide:code:ai} covers the tools.

\subsection{The contribution statement and the advisors' letter}\label{guide:writing:ai:contribution}
After the bibliography, the template includes an \emph{Eredménynyilatkozat} (\path{content/contribution-statement.tex}), the advisors' message (\path{include/advisor-ai-letter.tex}), and the faculty's AI declaration, in that order. All three belong to the reviewer PDF. We add the contribution statement and the letter as advisors; they are not extra faculty forms. The statement explains who did the work, the letter explains our supervision and checks, and the declaration records AI use in detail.

Write the contribution statement against the contributions listed in the introduction. For each result, identify what you designed or decided, what you implemented or investigated, and what evidence supports it. Point to the relevant sections and artifacts. Identify the contribution of each coauthor, your advisors and other collaborators, and cite reused methods, code and data. State when the research goal or initial solution was developed together with an advisor. Do not turn shared work into a claim of sole authorship.

Describe AI assistance alongside that division of work. A generated line of code or sentence does not measure the intellectual work behind a result. Requirements, choice of method, rejected alternatives, experimental design, interpretation and verification can be substantial student contributions even when a tool produces much of the implementation. Conversely, accepting a generated solution without understanding or checking it does not establish an independent contribution. Explain the actual decisions and checks so the reviewer can assess them.

Our letter records the working method we encouraged. AI tools may suggest directions and alternatives, support prototypes, and carry out bounded tasks. Socratic dialogue can expose assumptions through questions and counterexamples; a suggestion becomes a research result only through its development and justification. Students must formulate tasks and requirements, compare alternatives and justify decisions with the tools' failure modes in mind. This applies to code, models, experiment infrastructure, analysis scripts and guided development of formalisms.

Match verification to the importance of the task and the consequences of error. Claims, calculations and implementations that determine the results need an independent basis, such as a checked derivation, a primary source, a reproducible experiment or tests against a justified specification. Another AI answer, including agreement between models, is not independent evidence. Correct identified errors and resolve uncertainties or state them as limitations. AI assistance does not lower the standard of correctness.

Develop at least the argument and outline of the main text yourself. AI can help express it in English and revise the language. It can also turn your figure sketch into a finished figure. In both cases, check that the final output preserves the intended technical content.

The letter also states that the advisors personally checked the essential solution ideas, result-determining code, mathematical claims and derivations, and accept professional responsibility within the paper's stated assumptions and scope. It is an account of completed supervision. Review it with the advisors before submission: they must confirm that these statements describe the checks they actually performed. The student remains responsible for the submitted content, as the faculty declaration says. The letter does not waive faculty requirements or replace the supervisor's decision about whether the essential work is the student's own.

\subsection{Fill in the faculty declaration}
Keep the faculty's Hungarian wording in \tplref{aidecl}. Mark exactly one box. For every applicable use, give the tool and version, affected chapters, pages or figures, the estimated share and the essential part of the prompt; put -- in unused rows. Include the date and a conversation link where possible. Add a \emph{Magyarázat} row below a prompt when needed to explain how you counted the share, following the faculty's filled-in example in \path{docs/VIK_Generativ_MI_Pelda_Nyilatkozat_Szakdolgozathoz.pdf}.

Give the overall share of the substantive work and justify it. Explain the denominator and how overlapping uses were counted; do not simply add percentages for code, prose and ideas. The percentages describe use. The contribution statement supplies the account of scientific work needed to interpret them.

Record an AI answer in its use row with a one-sentence prompt summary, model and version, company, date and, if possible, a conversation link. The faculty's example is \enquote{Examples of harm-reduction initiatives} prompt. ChatGPT-4o, OpenAI, February 10, 2025. Under our citation rule above, this entry stays in the declaration; facts and conclusions in the paper cite the primary sources that verify them. A thesis is signed on the printed page. For TDK papers, the template prints the date and name without a signature line, according to \verb|\vikdoktipus|.

\paragraph{Keep every conversation.} Save every AI conversation about the work. Do not use temporary or incognito chats. The declaration needs the essential part of each prompt; an AI citation needs its date and a link. Transcripts also let you recover decisions, arguments and text. Check that your tools retain them, and do not delete them.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Claude Code keeps every session as a transcript under \path{~/.claude/projects/}, one file per session; Codex keeps them under \path{~/.codex/sessions/}.
  \item ChatGPT and Claude on the web keep conversations in your account history; create a share link for any conversation you cite.

These transcripts can contain everything you pasted in, so keep them out of public repositories.

\paragraph{Do not wipe your machine.} During the TDK, avoid reinstalling the operating system or clearing your home directory. If necessary, first back up \path{~/.claude/}, \path{~/.codex/}, \path{~/.t3/} and any other tool histories, then restore them afterwards.


\section{Cutting a paper to its page budget}\label{guide:writing:cutting}

Measure the submission build with \texttt{make final}, then run
\texttt{make check-final}. Count the main text as defined in
\cref{guide:tdk}; record the commit, the first and last counted pages, and the
chapter boundaries from the PDF. Draft notes and the guide are not part of
that budget. Moving glossary tables behind the bibliography does not save
main-text pages when they were already outside the counted interval.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Give each chapter a budget before editing. Reserve room for the
  contribution, its assumptions, the evaluation and the limitations. The
  sum must fit the main-text limit; a long approach chapter cannot consume
  the space needed to substantiate its claims.
  \item Cut repetition first. Keep each definition, design rationale and
  result at one authoritative location, and refer to it elsewhere. A
  running example should advance at each appearance, rather than restart.
  Remove background that no later argument uses.
  \item Move reference material to an appendix: complete grammars, API and
  error catalogues, long derivations, additional traces and full result
  tables. Keep the statement, its conditions, the reason it matters and a
  precise appendix reference in the main text. Keep the evidence needed to
  judge the contribution there too. An appendix must not hide a limitation
  or supply a missing central argument.
  \item Choose between redundant presentations. A diagram, table, listing
  and paragraph need not all describe the same control flow. Keep the form
  that explains the decision best; let the prose interpret it. Preserve
  distinct measurements and uncertainty when condensing result tables.
  \item Tighten layout after cutting content. Crop empty figure margins,
  allow sensible float placement, shorten table cells and use adjacent
  panels when their labels remain readable. Do not force every float
  exactly here. Keep the required margins, body font and line spacing;
  respect the figure and table readability rule in \cref{guide:tdk}. Avoid
  negative spacing and scaling a whole table until its text is too small.
\end{enumerate}

For each proposed cut, record its source label, the action (delete,
condense or move), what must remain, its destination and any references
that must be repaired. Estimated savings are planning figures until a
rebuilt PDF confirms them; float movement means they do not add exactly.
Apply one group of edits at a time, rebuild and inspect the changed pages
for whitespace, clipped content, unresolved references and unreadable
labels. Stop when the argument fits, then rerun the submission checks.
Keep the author's notes and outstanding gaps visible in the draft.
